A duck that falls in under a second

An AI can write a poem in a few seconds. Microduck, a robot duck about 25 cm tall, was told to hold its standing pose in a simulator and then let go. In less than a second it was lying on the floor. If AI is so good with language, why is staying upright so hard once it has a body? This course starts with that question and ends with this duck walking on its own.

Physical AI: AI with a body

Most of the AI we use takes data in and sends data out. Physical AI has a body: it senses the real world and acts in it. Roboticists don’t agree on one definition of a robot, but two ingredients come up again and again (Herath and St-Onge 2022, Ch. 1):

  • Intelligence — not human-like cleverness, but making decisions on its own from the limited information it has
  • Embodiment — a real physical body, not only a program in a computer

The body is what makes everything harder. A poem with a wrong word can be erased and rewritten. A robot that decides wrongly mid-step has already fallen, and there is no undo. It can’t take its time either: Microduck makes a new decision 50 times every second, because gravity never pauses.

Moving is not the same as sensing

Ducks have been machines before. In the 18th century the French inventor Vaucanson built a mechanical duck that drank, dabbled and quacked (Herath and St-Onge 2022, sec. 1.4). It was an automaton: like a music box, it played the same sequence every time. Roll a ball into it and it carries on with the same motion, because it senses nothing. Microduck measures its own body and the world all the time and changes what it does based on what it measures. That is the line between a machine that moves and a robot.

Sense, think, act

This course looks at every robot as a loop of three steps:

  • Sense: sensors measure the world or the robot’s own body
  • Think: decide what to do
  • Act: actuators, such as motors, move the body; the world changes, so the robot measures again

A robot vacuum at home is the everyday example: the front bumper is pressed by a table leg (sense), it chooses to turn right (think), the wheels turn it away (act). Microduck runs the same loop much faster: an IMU that measures which way the body tilts plus the angle of every joint, a learned policy, and 14 motors in the legs, neck and head.

Why walking is hard

The textbook’s Stanford Cart (1979) stopped to scan its surroundings for 15 minutes after every metre and crossed a 20-metre room in five hours (Herath and St-Onge 2022, sec. 1.6). A cart can stop and think, because a four-wheeled vehicle that stands still doesn’t fall. A two-legged robot that stops to think mid-step falls before it has finished thinking.

Standing still isn’t doing nothing, either. Stand on one leg and your ankle keeps making small corrections. That is exactly what Microduck lacked in the opening shot: every joint held its pose, nothing corrected the body as it started to tilt, and it fell after 0.86 s. Lab 3 reproduces this fall.

The course path

  1. The body (Season 1): where is the robot, and how do we make a joint move — in the MuJoCo simulator
  2. Learning (Season 2): write agents that learn from reward
  3. Microduck (Season 3): train it to walk and test how robust it is

Why a simulator first? Like a pilot in a flight simulator, the robot can fall as often as it likes and nothing breaks, and time runs much faster than in the real world. Moving a trained policy onto a real robot, sim-to-real, is a hard step of its own. This year the course ends in simulation; that is not a shortcut, since nearly all RL work on real legged robots is trained and screened in simulation first. The tools: Python to write code, Colab to run notebooks in the browser, and Git to keep a history of every change.

What the labs showed

Lab 1 — Drive Microduck in the browser: in Pollen Robotics’ simulator the W key only says “I want to go forward”; which foot to lift and where to put it, the policy decides from its sensors, 50 times a second (CTRL 50HZ on screen). Kicking with Q, the other leg alone carries the body; sitting and standing use a second learned policy.

Lab 2 — Python, Git and Colab: with Microduck’s numbers, dt = 1 / 50 gives one decision every 0.02 s, 3000 decisions per minute, and 43 decisions in the 0.86 s it took to fall. NumPy converts the left leg’s standing pose from radians to degrees in one command: [0, -5, -26.2, -0.3, 26]. Git saves the notes as a first commit, through working tree → staging area → repository (Herath and St-Onge 2022, sec. 4.8).

Lab 3 — Open Microduck in MuJoCo and watch it fall: the model has 15 joints, 14 motors and 6 sensors. The extra joint, trunk_base_freejoint, lets the trunk move freely in the world and has no motor. No sensor says directly where the feet are. Holding the STAND pose, the trunk sinks slowly from about 12 cm, then drops below 9 cm at 0.86 s and lands at about 5 cm. With stiffer servos (kp 0.55 → 2.0) its lowest point is 11.5 cm and it stays up for the whole 1.5 s — but that is holding the pose better, not measuring and correcting.

Review questions

  1. What two ingredients make something a robot rather than a program, and why does the body make decisions harder?
  2. Why was Vaucanson’s duck an automaton and not a robot?
  3. For a robot vacuum, name the sense, think and act steps when it bumps into a table leg.
  4. Why could the Stanford Cart stop for 15 minutes when a biped can’t stop mid-step?
  5. For each task, pick rules, control or learning: a light at 6 pm; holding a joint at 30°; walking on rough ground.
  6. In Lab 2, how many decisions does a 50 Hz policy make in one hour? (The Lab 2 exercise.)
  7. In Lab 3, why doesn’t trunk_base_freejoint have a motor, and why isn’t kp 2.0 real balancing?
